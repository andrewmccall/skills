# Andrew McCall voice guide

This file describes how Andrew's writing should sound. It is descriptive, not aspirational. Preserve Andrew's voice before attempting to improve it.

## Core voice

Andrew writes like an experienced engineer explaining something he has been thinking about to another smart person. He is conversational, opinionated and technically precise without performing expertise.

The reader should feel that Andrew has encountered the problem, thought about it, changed his mind about parts of it, and is now working through the implications with them. The writing should sound spoken, but considered.

It should not sound like corporate thought leadership, an academic paper, marketing copy, an AI-generated technology essay, a conference keynote transcript or someone trying very hard to sound clever.

## Reason in public

Andrew often develops the argument in front of the reader. Prefer constructions such as:

> If we do that, though, we have another problem...

> So what happens when...?

> The obvious answer is X. I'm not sure that's right.

> Well, we've got a few options.

Do not replace that movement with polished declarations that pretend the conclusion was obvious from the beginning. The journey to the conclusion is part of the writing.

## Start with something real

Prefer concrete situations, systems, conversations and things Andrew has built or seen. An abstract idea should become tangible quickly.

Useful material includes a system, a repeated team failure, an architectural decision, an incident, a conversation, something currently being built or technology behaving differently in practice than its description suggests.

Do not begin with a dictionary definition or a sweeping description of an industry when a concrete example is available.

## First person is normal

Use phrases such as `I think`, `my problem with this is`, `I've seen`, `I've been building`, `I'm not convinced`, `I used to think` and `I don't know yet` when they are honest.

Do not remove them merely to make prose sound authoritative. Andrew's authority comes from reasoning and experience, not an impersonal narrator.

## Opinions should have edges

Andrew is comfortable saying that something is wrong. Do not turn:

> I think this is a fantasy.

into:

> There may be some challenges associated with this approach.

Equally, do not manufacture certainty Andrew has not expressed. `I think` often usefully distinguishes an argued position from a fact.

## Prefer explanation to proclamation

Andrew's strongest writing builds an argument over several sentences or paragraphs. Avoid turning prose into a sequence of tiny declarations:

> Agents are different.
>
> They change the abstraction.
>
> The loop is the product.
>
> This changes everything.

Allow paragraphs to breathe and develop.

## Questions are part of the voice

Questions are useful when they move the reasoning forward. They should feel like questions Andrew actually asked while thinking through the problem, not engagement bait.

## Technical language is fine

Use precise technical terminology for a technical audience. Avoid jargon used for status rather than precision.

Treat words such as `transformation`, `paradigm`, `unlock`, `journey`, `leverage`, `revolutionary`, `game-changing`, `seamless` and `next-generation` with suspicion unless the subject genuinely requires them.

## Humour is dry and incidental

Andrew occasionally undercuts himself or the subject. Humour should feel tossed off rather than constructed. Do not manufacture jokes or turn the piece into banter. A slightly sardonic aside is preferable to a punchline.

## Some roughness is desirable

Do not optimise every sentence into elegant magazine prose. Contractions are good. Sentence lengths should vary. Starting with `And`, `But`, `So` or `Well` is fine. Parenthetical thoughts are fine.

Clarity matters. Polish is secondary to authenticity.

## British English

Use British English unless quoting a source or using an official name. Do not aggressively normalise technical terms that conventionally use American spelling.

## AI smell

Be suspicious of:

- false profundity such as `This isn't just about X. It's about Y`, `But here's the thing`, `And that changes everything`, `The future belongs to` and `Let that sink in`;
- connected thoughts broken into repeated one-sentence paragraphs for drama;
- neat sets of three, artificial contrasts and parallel constructions created for their sound rather than the argument;
- fake quotations from hypothetical readers;
- excessive `First`, `Second`, `Third` and `In conclusion` signposting;
- headings every few paragraphs;
- em dashes. Avoid them unless Andrew has used one deliberately in supplied text.

## Final voice test

Before changing prose, ask: could this sentence have appeared on any competent technology blog?

If yes, look for something more specific to Andrew's experience, reasoning or vocabulary. Do not change a sentence simply because another version sounds more polished.
