# Andrew McCall writing style

This file defines what strong writing in Andrew's profile should accomplish. `VOICE.md` protects how Andrew sounds. `STYLE.md` makes the thinking stronger.

Not every piece needs every element. These are diagnostic tools, not a template.

## The fundamental rule

A piece needs an argument or purpose, not merely a subject.

`Agent evals` is a subject. `Agent evals are becoming the new requirements engineering` is an idea. `The hard part of autonomous agents isn't building the loop. It's specifying what done means, which is a problem software engineering has been failing at for decades` is an argument.

Before drafting, be able to answer:

- What do I believe or need the reader to understand?
- What does the conventional view miss?
- Why does this matter to this reader?

If the answers are not interesting yet, do not compensate with prose. Develop the idea.

For a factual memo or design document, the purpose may be a decision rather than a disputable thesis. State what decision the document makes possible and what trade-off the reader must understand.

## Find the itch

Good writing often begins because something does not quite sit right. Identify the disagreement, contradiction, hidden complexity, missing responsibility, gap between theory and practice or old problem wearing a new name.

Write down the itch before drafting when the piece is exploratory. For a requested business document, identify the unresolved decision or consequence instead.

## Find the strongest claim

Ask for the strongest version the author actually believes. Do not automatically publish that formulation; use it to discover what the piece is really about.

If no reasonable reader could disagree with an argumentative thesis, it may be too bland. If the document is primarily factual, ask what its facts change.

## Earn the argument

Opinion is useful. Unsupported opinion is less interesting. Look for direct experience, a concrete system, an implementation detail, historical precedent, data, external research, an opposing example, a failure mode and the strongest counterargument.

Prefer showing why something is true over repeatedly asserting it. Clearly distinguish fact, inference, opinion and anecdote. Mark important factual claims that need verification rather than inventing support.

## Mine experience

Before reaching for generic examples, ask whether Andrew has encountered the problem. Useful prompts include:

- When have you seen this happen?
- What did the team think at the time?
- What actually went wrong?
- What surprised you?
- What would you do differently now?
- What does this remind you of from ten years ago?
- Have you built anything that supports or contradicts this?

Experience should do argumentative work, not serve as credential waving.

## Give the piece progression

The reader should not merely receive a thesis followed by five supporting points. Something should develop.

A useful progression is:

```text
observation
    -> concrete example
    -> apparently obvious explanation
    -> complication
    -> more useful interpretation
    -> consequence or decision
```

This is movement, not a mandatory article structure. A design document may instead move from constraint to options to trade-off to decision. A proposal may move from current cost to opportunity to credible plan. Preserve the format the task needs.

## Look for the turn

Strong pieces often contain a moment where the interpretation changes. We may think we are discussing data quality and discover that we are discussing ownership. We may think evals are a new AI discipline and discover that much of the hard part resembles requirements engineering.

Do not manufacture the turn. Find one already present in the material.

## Preserve useful tension

Do not immediately resolve every contradiction. If two things are simultaneously true, explore that. Agent evals can be essential while teams remain poor at specifying them.

## Steelman before attacking

When criticising an approach, describe its strongest reasonable version. Ask why smart people believe it, when they would be right, and which part Andrew agrees with. A stronger opposing argument makes the resulting position more credible.

## So what?

At least once, assume the piece is completely right and ask what changes. Could it change a design, a team practice, a responsibility, an investment, a measurement or a decision?

If nothing follows, the piece may be interesting but not yet consequential.

## Leave something memorable

Do not manufacture a slogan. Look for the simplest formulation of the piece's most useful idea. A memorable concept is better than a sentence performing memorability.

## Openings

Prefer an observation, experience, current project, disagreement, concrete problem or genuine question. Avoid broad historical throat-clearing, dictionary definitions, `In today's rapidly evolving...`, unnecessary background for a knowledgeable audience and executive summaries that give away every discovery.

Get into the problem quickly.

## Endings

Do not summarise the entire piece by default. Expose the larger implication, return to the opening with changed meaning, state what Andrew now believes, explain what changes next or leave an unresolved but useful question.

Stop when the argument or decision has landed. Do not add an inspirational paragraph merely because the ending feels abrupt.

## Readiness test

- [ ] I can state why this piece exists.
- [ ] Its point of view, purpose or decision is clear.
- [ ] Important claims are supported or visibly marked for verification.
- [ ] There is something concrete in the piece.
- [ ] The reasoning develops rather than repeats.
- [ ] The strongest relevant objection or trade-off has been considered.
- [ ] The reader understands why this matters and what follows.
- [ ] At least one idea is worth remembering.
- [ ] The ending adds something rather than merely summarising.
- [ ] The result still sounds like Andrew.
